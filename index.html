    Common questions

    Can you build for both iOS and Android?

    Yes. Where the platform allows it we share a codebase to keep behaviour identical and reduce maintenance, and we drop to native code only where the shared layer would compromise a requirement such as background location or a hardware-backed keychain.

    How do you handle offline use?

    We decide the sync model during the prototype phase, not after a field complaint. Typically that means a local queue with an explicit conflict rule and a visible sync state, so the user always knows whether their work is saved.

    Do you take over an app our team already started?

    Commonly, yes. We start with a short audit of the codebase and infrastructure, give you a written assessment, and only then propose work. You own the assessment document whether or not you continue with us.

    Design validated against real accessibility standards and real users, before production code is written.

    • Research, wireframes and interactive prototypes
    • WCAG 2.2 AA colour, contrast and keyboard support
    • Reusable design tokens with component documentation

    A design system is not a picture of a library. It is the set of decisions — colour, type, spacing, motion, focus behaviour — that lets a team ship a new screen without a designer in the room and still get something consistent.

    We start with the product you have, not a green-field idealisation. An audit of your current interface usually surfaces the same three or four problems repeatedly: inconsistent spacing, three competing button styles, and a form pattern nobody can complete on a phone. Fixing those produces more value than a new visual language.

    From there we build a token layer — colour, typography, spacing, radius, elevation and motion — and a component set on top of it. Everything is specified against WCAG 2.2 AA, which in practice means contrast checked against real text, visible focus on every interactive element, and full keyboard operability. We test with assistive technology rather than claiming compliance from a colour checker.

    Common questions

    Is this a designer-only engagement?

    Not usually. The most reliable outcomes come when design and front-end engineering sit together, because tokens and components are only enforced when they exist in the codebase. We can supply design only if that is what your team needs.

    What does "WCAG 2.2 AA" actually cover here?

    In our work it means text contrast checked against real content, visible focus indicators, complete keyboard operability, correct semantics and labels, and testing with a screen reader. Colour choice alone does not make a product accessible.

    Will the system work if we change designers?

    That is the point. The system, the tokens and the documented component rules outlive any individual designer, and Storybook gives your engineers a reference they can read without asking.

    Common questions

    Will you work in our cloud, or push us to yours?

    Whichever you already run. Migrating because a partner prefers a different cloud is a cost with no matching benefit. We are equally comfortable in AWS and Azure and will say so if a service we would choose is materially more expensive for your workload.

    How do you reduce cloud spend?

    We measure before we change anything: rightsizing, storage lifecycle policies, idle resource sweeps, commitment discounts sized against actual steady-state usage, and architecture changes where they genuinely beat tuning. The majority of savings usually come from the first two, not from moving workloads.

    Do you hand over the infrastructure?

    Yes. Accounts stay in your organisation, the code is in your repository, and we run a handover session plus a short period of parallel on-call so your team is comfortable before we step back.

    APIs, events and data sync that hold up under load and under audit.

    • REST, GraphQL and event-driven integration patterns
    • Legacy, ERP and third-party system connectivity
    • Idempotency, retries and full audit trails

    Almost every mid-sized company runs a stack nobody would choose today: a CRM from 2014, an ERP with a SOAP interface, a payments provider with a webhook, and a warehouse that updates nightly. The value is rarely in replacing that. It is in making it work together.

    We design integration as a product surface, with versioning, contracts, idempotency and observability designed in from the start. That discipline is what separates an integration that works in a demo from one that is still trustworthy three years later, when nobody remembers who wrote it.

    Where the data needs to move continuously we prefer events over polling, and we make failure explicit: dead-letter queues, replay tooling, and a reconciliation job that tells you when two systems disagree. We also write the operational documentation, because an integration nobody can debug at 2am is a liability regardless of how well it works at noon.

    Common questions

    Can you integrate a system that has no modern API?

    Usually. Screen-scraping is a last resort, not a first choice, but fixed-width files, database views, SOAP and even message queues can all be wrapped in a clean adapter with a proper interface in front. We tell you honestly when the only remaining option is a screen-scraper and what that will cost to maintain.

    How do you handle rate limits and flaky third parties?

    Every outbound call is wrapped with a circuit breaker, exponential backoff and jitter, and the result is written to a durable queue rather than retried inline. A third-party outage degrades to "queued", not "down".

    Do you document the integration?

    Yes — a data-flow diagram, an endpoint catalogue and runbooks for the failure modes that actually happen. It is part of the deliverable, not a favour.

    Warehouse, modelling and dashboards built on a definition everyone agrees on.

    • Warehouse, ELT pipelines and modelled marts
    • Metric definitions owned centrally and versioned
    • Self-service dashboards with row-level access

    Most "we need a data platform" requests are really "the sales number and the finance number are different and nobody can say which is right". That is a definitions problem before it is a technology problem, and it needs solving in that order.

    We start by agreeing the metric definitions and putting them in one place — active customer, churned account, recognised revenue — with a single owner and a version history. Dashboards are then generated from those definitions rather than each team writing its own SQL, which is where the disagreement started.

    Underneath we build an ELT pipeline that is idempotent and observable, incremental rather than a nightly full reload, with tests that fail the build when a source schema changes or row counts move implausibly. The result is a warehouse people trust enough to build the company plan on.

    Incremental replacement around a strangler facade, so the business keeps trading throughout.

    • Strangler-pattern migration with no big-bang cutover
    • Characterisation tests around the parts you cannot rewrite
    • Domain-by-domain cutover with rollback at each step

    The worst thing you can do with a working legacy system is stop it working. Every modernisation that goes badly shares one cause: the team tried to replace the whole thing in one step, under business pressure, with the old system as the only thing that worked.

    We take the opposite approach. We put a facade in front of the legacy system, migrate one domain at a time, and keep the old path available until the new one has proved itself in production. Each domain cutover is small, reversible and scheduled at a point where the business can absorb it.

    Before we change anything we write characterisation tests around the existing behaviour, including the parts that look like bugs. That is what lets us refactor with confidence, and it is also how we find the undocumented business rules that a rewrite would silently drop. Where a rewrite genuinely is the right answer we will say so — but we will say it with evidence, and we will still stage the migration.

    Common questions

    How do you know the old system actually works as documented?

    We do not assume it does. We write characterisation tests against the running system, and the difference between documented and actual behaviour is usually the most valuable output of the first month.

    Is a rewrite ever the right answer?

    Sometimes, but only when the system is small enough to rewrite in months rather than years, or when it is genuinely blocking every change. We will give you a straight recommendation with the reasoning, and the cost of being wrong.

    Will we keep trading during the migration?

    Yes. That is the defining constraint of this approach. Each cutover moves one domain, the previous path stays available, and we roll back if error rates or reconciliation totals move outside agreed thresholds.
